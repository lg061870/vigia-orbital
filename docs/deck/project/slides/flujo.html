How do people compute these indicators today? NASA’s ARSET program teaches it in its online course “Using the Fire Mapping Tools to Map Fires”: a manual workflow in QGIS, with Landsat images, one fire at a time. You pick the area, find the images before and after the fire, mask the clouds, calculate NBR before and after, subtract them to get dNBR, and classify the burn severity. It’s powerful, but it answers one question: how badly did it burn, after the fact. Vigía Orbital automates that whole chain: every new MODIS image, cloud-free pixels only, NDVI, NDMI, NBR and dNBR for every canton since 2012, each compared with what’s normal for that date, together with NASA FIRMS fire detections and NASA POWER weather. About 2,000 MODIS image composites and almost 158,000 fire detections. And on top of all that data, a machine-learning model that learns from 14 fire seasons and forecasts the next five days. Tested on seasons it never saw, it beats an average year by 8% for tomorrow and still by 5% on day five. So the question changes from “how badly did it burn?” to “where and when is fire likely, before it happens?”
